CHAPTER I — THE PROBLEM AND ITS SCOPE

Introduction

Laundry is a regular household task in many Filipino communities, and small laundry shops have become part of the daily routine for families, students, and workers. Lavhanan Laundry Hub is a locally owned laundry business owned and operated by Viema Joy E. Villafuerte, located at 91P Burgos St., Sindangan, Zamboanga del Norte. It is open from Monday to Saturday, 7:30 a.m. to 7:00 p.m., and offers full wash, premium full wash, wash-dry-fold packages, wash-only service, quick and premium drying, folding, and detergent or fabric-conditioner add-ons.

Like many small businesses, Lavhanan records customer orders in a handwritten logbook and issues small paper receipts. The cashier writes the customer's name, the service, the weight of the clothes, the item count, and the amount by hand. The laundry worker then updates the progress of each batch informally, and the owner totals the day's earnings from the receipts at closing time. This workflow works while the volume is low, but it becomes difficult to manage as the number of customers grows. Records are hard to search, item counts are easy to dispute, unpaid or unclaimed orders are easy to forget, and the daily sales total depends on manual arithmetic.

This project designs and prototypes a web-based Laundry Hub Management System for Lavhanan. The system keeps customer and order records in a database, records exact clothing counts, tracks each order through washing, drying, folding, and ready-for-pickup, records payment status, prints a thermal receipt, sends automatic SMS notifications, and produces daily and monthly sales reports. It is intended to support the shop's existing workflow rather than replace the business. The goal is to give the owner and staff a faster, more accurate way to manage records while giving customers a simple way to check the status of their laundry.

The Problem

The business currently relies on manual recording and coordination. This creates five specific problems:

  1. Handwritten records are difficult to keep and search. Orders, customer details, and payments are written in a logbook and on small paper receipts. Old records are hard to find, pages can be lost, and there is no reliable way to search by customer name, phone number, or ticket code.

  2. Clothing counts are not verified. Staff do not always count every piece of clothing. When a customer later claims that an item is missing, the shop has no written count to check against, which can lead to disputes and lost trust.

  3. Unpaid and unclaimed orders require manual follow-up. Staff must search paper records for the customer's phone number and call or text customers one by one. Busy days make this easy to postpone, so laundry stays on the shelf longer than necessary.

  4. Daily sales are added up by hand. The owner totals the day's receipts manually. This is slow, prone to arithmetic errors, and makes it difficult to see monthly trends or compare services.

  5. Customers have no direct way to check their laundry status. Customers call or visit the shop to ask whether their clothes are ready. This interrupts staff work and gives customers no estimate of when to return.

Beneficiaries

The system is designed for the following beneficiaries:

| Beneficiary | Benefit |

| ----------- | ------- |

| Owner / Admin | Sees daily and monthly sales automatically, manages staff accounts, monitors all orders, and reviews the SMS outbox. |

| Manager | Sees all orders and customer details, monitors progress, and resolves customer problems without access to financial reports. |

| Cashier | Records orders faster, captures exact item counts, prints a receipt, and marks orders paid or unpaid. |

| Labandera (laundry worker) | Uses one-click status buttons and sees a clear washing queue instead of relying on memory or verbal updates. |

| Customers | Check their own ticket status, clothing counts, and amount due from a phone; receive an SMS when the laundry is ready. |

| Delivery driver (future) | Will be able to see the customer's address and mark a delivery as completed. |

| The business | Gains accurate records, fewer disputes, faster customer service, and data for planning. |

Scope and Limitations of the Design

Scope. The design covers a responsive web application with role-based access for Owner/Admin, Manager, Cashier, Labandera, and Customer. It includes:

  • customer and order management with service selection, weight, load count, and exact clothing item counts;

  • an order lifecycle of Received → Washing → Drying → Folding → Ready → Claimed;

  • paid/unpaid payment tracking, with a rule that an order with an unpaid balance cannot be deleted;

  • a printable thermal receipt;

  • automatic SMS notifications for ready-for-pickup, unclaimed reminders, and power-outage broadcasts;

  • daily, monthly, and custom-range sales reports with CSV export;

  • account management, profile/password changes, and a role-specific help page;

  • a customer portal that shows only the logged-in customer's own tickets.

Limitations. The prototype and design have the following limits:

  • The delivered prototype is a responsive web application. It runs in a browser on Android phones, tablets, and computers. A native Android application is part of the design direction but is not a delivered artifact in this prototype; there is no iOS native application.

  • SMS delivery in the prototype uses a mock outbox transport so the flow can be demonstrated offline. A production deployment must connect a real SMS gateway (for example, Twilio or Vonage) and requires an internet connection.

  • Thermal printing uses the browser's 72 mm print layout and the printer's own driver. The system does not send raw ESC/POS commands directly to the printer.

  • The system does not process online payments, scan barcodes or RFID tags, read a digital weighing scale, or support multiple branches. These are possible future extensions.

  • The server is intended to stay at the shop. Staff devices on the local Wi-Fi can continue working during an internet outage, but SMS notifications and remote customer access require internet.

  • The SUKI CARD loyalty feature (10 stamps → 11th wash free) is a future enhancement, not part of the current build.

  • The prototype uses seeded demo accounts and fictional customer names. Real customer data must be handled under the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173).

CHAPTER II — REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES

Introduction to the Review

This chapter reviews literature and studies related to laundry and service-business management, SMS-based customer notifications, order-status tracking, role-based access control, and systems development models. The sources were selected based on their relevance to small-business information systems, customer communication, and secure multi-role web applications.

Related Literature

Laundry and Service-Business Management Systems

Small service businesses improve their daily operations by shifting from manual recordkeeping to computerized management systems. Zulkifli et al. (2021) reviewed mobile pickup-and-delivery applications for laundry services and described how these systems organise customer requests, order details, and service schedules in one place. Adetya (2024) developed a web-based laundry management system following the Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC), while Anwar et al. (2026) built a laundry information system for small and medium enterprises using the waterfall model, covering user roles, order tracking, and reporting. Together these studies show that even small shops benefit once order intake and customer data move from paper into a shared database.

The reviewed systems also emphasise keeping accurate records and producing reports from them. Kurniawati et al. (2026) described a laundry system with audit trails intended to reduce recording irregularities, and Uddin et al. (2022) developed an Android-and-web application that records item details, including images, when an order is accepted. Industry practice in the Philippine market points the same way: LabadaTech (2026) presents an online laundry service where customers book, track, and pay for orders through a web platform, showing that local shops already promote digital order handling to their customers (Adetya, 2024; Anwar et al., 2026; Kurniawati et al., 2026; LabadaTech, 2026; Uddin et al., 2022; Zulkifli et al., 2021).

The studies above span desktop database builds, server-rendered web applications, and Android-native or mobile-only apps. Lavhanan Laundry Hub delivers a responsive web application that runs in the browser on Android phones, tablets, and PCs, with a native Android client left as future work.

SMS Notifications and Automated Customer Reminders

Text messaging is a practical notification channel for small service businesses because it reaches the customer's own phone directly and needs no application installed on the customer's side. Cabañero (2023) described a laundry booking system that connects to an SMS gateway, so a customer receives a text message about the status of an order instead of calling the shop to ask. In the local market, EngagePOS Philippines (2026) offers a similar capability in its laundry point-of-sale product, where a text message is sent to the customer once an order is ready for pickup (Cabañero, 2023; EngagePOS Philippines, 2026).

These examples show that automated messages are used for exactly the two events this project needs to announce: an order becoming ready, and a reminder for an order that has stayed unclaimed. The material reviewed also makes the limits of the channel visible, since a text message is short, depends on a working gateway and internet connection, and needs a valid mobile number before it can be sent.

Order-Status Tracking and Customer Self-Service

Giving customers a way to see the progress of their own order reduces the number of follow-up questions the counter has to answer. Nugraha and Alfarid (2025) developed a web-based laundry system that displays order status in real time, so the customer can check the current stage of the process without asking staff. Andini and Zakariyah (2024) built a mobile application that lets customers monitor their position in a service queue and the status of their request (Andini & Zakariyah, 2024; Nugraha & Alfarid, 2025).

Both studies place the status view on the customer's own device and restrict it to that customer's transaction. That is the same arrangement used in this project, where the customer portal shows only the signed-in customer's tickets while staff screens carry the status controls.

Role-Based Access Control and Data Privacy

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) assigns permissions to users according to their roles and responsibilities. Sandhu, Coyne, Feinstein, and Youman (1996) presented RBAC models that help organize user permissions and restrict access to necessary information. In the Lavhanan Laundry Hub, this is implemented through five roles: Owner/Admin, Manager, Cashier, Labandera (laundry worker), and Customer. Each role has specific permissions, such as allowing customers to view only their own orders, workers to update laundry processes, and only the Owner/Admin to access sales reports and account management. A Delivery Driver role is a future extension, not part of the current prototype. This helps prevent unauthorized access and protects sensitive records.

Since the system stores customer information, such as names, contact numbers, and order histories, it must follow the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). The Act establishes principles for lawful, transparent, and secure personal information processing and recognizes the rights of data subjects. To support these principles, the project uses fictional records for demonstrations and applies hashed passwords, CSRF protection, role-based access restrictions, and a customer-order voter. It also prevents orders with unpaid balances from being deleted to maintain proper transaction records.

Systems Development Models

This project adopted the V-model as its systems development model. Forsberg and Mooz (1991) described a systems engineering approach that connects project development stages with corresponding verification and validation activities. It emphasizes the relationship between requirements, system design, implementation, and testing. This approach suits the Lavhanan Laundry Hub because its requirements were gathered from an existing laundry business and must be checked against the system's implemented functions.

Pressman and Maxim (2020) discuss software engineering activities such as requirements analysis, system design, implementation, and testing as essential parts of software development. Their work supports the use of a structured development process in the Lavhanan Laundry Hub, where system requirements guide development and testing. Unlike a pure waterfall process, which moves strictly from one phase to the next, or an agile process, which relies on short iterative cycles, the V-model keeps a fixed sequence of phases while pairing each with its own verification step — a balance that suits an academic project whose requirements were fixed by the shop's actual workflow. Together, these references provide support for using a requirements-driven approach with defined verification and validation activities.

Synthesis

The review shows that small laundry and service businesses are moving from handwritten logbooks and paper receipts toward computerized management systems, and that this shift consistently improves record accuracy, order tracking, and reporting. Themes 1 and 3 demonstrate that digital order recording, real-time status tracking, and customer self-service portals reduce manual errors and inbound follow-up questions, while Theme 2 shows that SMS is a low-cost, effective channel for notifying customers when laundry is ready or left unclaimed. Theme 4 establishes that role-based access control and data-privacy safeguards are necessary whenever staff and customers share one system, and Theme 5 confirms that a requirements-driven lifecycle such as the V-model provides the traceability needed for an academic, shop-validated project. Taken together, the reviewed systems and studies converge on a common feature set — digital order recording, stage tracking, notifications, and reporting — but several are single-shop web prototypes with limited field validation, and some depend on continuous connectivity or foreign SaaS hosting.

The reviewed works also reveal persistent gaps that the proposed system must address. Exact clothing-item counting to prevent missing-item disputes is documented in only a few of the reviewed studies (for example, Uddin et al., 2022) and often through an approach that is heavy for a small shop; paid/unpaid tracking with protection against deleting unsettled orders is not consistently documented in the reviewed academic systems; SMS appears mainly as a booking-side add-on (Cabañero, 2023); offline resilience for a locally owned server is rarely addressed in the academic literature and only partially in local commercial tools (LabadaTech, 2026); and customer self-service is typically bundled into the same app as employee functions rather than offered as a simple separate view. Local, peso-based MSME deployment with a thermal receipt printer is documented only partially by local products (LabadaTech, 2026; EngagePOS Philippines, 2026).

The Lavhanan Laundry Hub Management System is therefore proposed to fill this documented gap for this specific business: a responsive web application (with a native Android client as future work) running on the shop's own local server, supporting five roles, recording exact item counts, tracking orders through washing, drying, folding, and ready-for-pickup, sending automated SMS for ready-for-pickup, unclaimed, and outage notices, producing daily and monthly sales reports, and printing 72 mm thermal receipts. Consistent with the declared scope, features from the richer commercial systems — inventory management, employee attendance, iOS support, and online payment gateways — are consciously excluded, since the prototype is a responsive web application, native Android and iOS clients remain future work, and the project scope does not cover online payments or supply inventory.

CHAPTER III — METHODOLOGY

Design

This project used the V-model as its systems development model. The V-model is a variation of the waterfall model that makes verification and validation explicit: each development phase on the left side is paired with a testing phase on the right side. This structure fits the project because the requirements were gathered from a real shop and needed to be traceable from design to testing.

Figure 1 - V-model.

Requirements Analysis

The team interviewed the owner and staff, observed the shop's workflow, and reviewed sample logbooks and receipts. The output was a list of functional and non-functional requirements: order recording, item counts, payment tracking, status updates, SMS notifications, sales reports, role-based access, and thermal receipt printing.

System Design

The requirements were translated into the system architecture, database design, and user-interface design. The logical design (context, use cases, classes, activity flow, ERD, data dictionary) and the physical design (architecture, deployment, components, UI, hardware) are presented in Chapter IV.

Implementation

The prototype was built with Symfony 8.1 (PHP 8.4+, developed on PHP 8.5), Doctrine ORM, MySQL 8, Twig, AssetMapper/Stimulus, Symfony Messenger, Symfony Notifier, and PHPUnit. Docker was used for the local database and mail services. Git tracked the milestones: core staff flow, customer portal, SMS notifications, and reports/extras.

Testing

Testing followed the right side of the V-model:

  • Unit testing — services and business rules (order totals, status transitions, payment rules, delete-block rule).

  • Integration testing — controllers, forms, Doctrine repositories, security voters, and the SMS outbox.

  • System testing — end-to-end flows for each role, including 403 checks and CSRF protection.

  • Acceptance testing — a demo script with the owner/staff using seeded data.

The automated suite covers 81 tests and 435 assertions and runs against a separate laundry_hub_test database. Manual smoke tests covered the browser flows, receipt printing, CSV export, and the SMS worker.

Instrument Use in Data Gathering

The team used three instruments:

  1. Semi-structured interview guide. Questions for the owner, manager, cashier, and labandera about the current workflow, pain points, reporting needs, customer communication, roles, and hardware.

  2. Observation checklist. A guided observation of order intake, weighing, receipt writing, status updates, pickup, and payment. The checklist recorded who performs each step, what documents are used, and where delays or errors occur.

  3. Document review. Sample logbook pages, receipts, the price list, and promo materials were reviewed to understand the data the system must capture.

An optional short questionnaire for customers can be added to validate the portal and SMS expectations.

Interview Guide (outline)

  • How do you record a new order today? What information do you write down?

  • How do you count the clothes, and what happens when a customer says an item is missing?

  • How do you know which orders are unpaid or unclaimed? What do you do about them?

  • How do you compute daily and monthly sales? How long does it take?

  • How do customers ask about their laundry status? How often?

  • Who uses the system, and what should each role be allowed to see or change?

  • What devices, printer, and internet connection are available at the shop?

  • What would make the system easy or difficult to use in daily work?

Data Gathering Procedures

  1. Permission and orientation. A letter of permission was presented to the owner, followed by a short orientation on the project's purpose and the voluntary nature of participation.

  2. Interviews. The team interviewed the owner and representative staff at the shop. Responses were transcribed and grouped by theme (records, counts, follow-up, reports, customer communication, security).

  3. Observation. The team observed the order-to-pickup workflow on [MISSING: observation dates], using the observation checklist. Notes focused on handoffs, delays, and duplicate work.

  4. Document review. Sample receipts, logbook pages, and the service price list were collected and compared with the interview responses.

  5. Requirements consolidation. The team converted the findings into user stories and functional requirements, then reviewed them with the owner for correction and approval.

  6. Validation. The prototype was demonstrated to the owner/staff using the acceptance script. Feedback was recorded and minor adjustments were made before the final documentation.

CHAPTER IV — PROJECT DESCRIPTION AND TECHNICAL BACKGROUND

Project Description

Lavhanan Laundry Hub is a web-based laundry management system designed for a small laundry business in Sindangan, Zamboanga del Norte. It replaces the handwritten logbook and paper receipt workflow with a shared database, role-based screens, printed thermal receipts, SMS notifications, and automatic sales reports.

Figure 2 - Map of the Target Environment.

The map shows the shop's location at 91P Burgos Street, Sindangan, Zamboanga del Norte.

The system serves five roles: Owner/Admin, Manager, Cashier, Labandera, and Customer. Staff roles use the management side; customers use a separate portal that shows only their own tickets. The Owner/Admin has the widest access, including accounts, financial reports, and the SMS outbox. The Manager sees all orders but not financial reports. The Cashier records orders and payments. The Labandera updates the laundry steps. The Customer views status, item counts, and amount due.

Class Diagram

  • Customer — name, phone, optional address, optional linked user account, orders.

Activity Diagram

The order lifecycle activity flow is:

  1. Cashier records the customer, service, weight, and clothing counts.

  2. System computes the total and generates a ticket code.

  3. Cashier prints the receipt and records payment as paid or unpaid.

  4. Labandera moves the order through Washing → Drying → Folding.

  5. When the order becomes Ready, the system queues an SMS to the customer.

  6. Customer claims the laundry; staff mark the order Claimed.

  7. If the order is unpaid, deletion is blocked until the balance is settled.

Physical Design

Architectural Diagram

The system follows a layered MVC architecture:

  • Client layer — desktop or mobile browser.

  • Presentation layer — Twig templates rendered by Symfony; AssetMapper serves CSS/JS; Stimulus handles the auto-refresh on the customer portal.

  • Application layer — controllers handle HTTP requests; services enforce business rules (OrderService, SmsNotificationService, UserService); security handles authentication, role checks, and the customer-order voter.

  • Domain layer — Doctrine entities and repositories.

  • Infrastructure layer — MySQL, Symfony Messenger, the Notifier outbox transport, and the mailer.

  • External services — SMS gateway (production) and the thermal printer (via the browser's print dialog).

Figure 5 - Architectural Diagram of the System.

Deployment Diagram

The prototype runs on a shop server or PC with Docker. The MySQL database, phpMyAdmin, and a local mail catcher run as containers. The Symfony application is served by PHP (the Symfony CLI server in development, or Apache/Nginx + PHP-FPM in production). Staff devices and customer phones connect over the shop's local Wi-Fi or the internet. SMS delivery leaves the shop network through the SMS gateway.

Figure 14 - Deployment Diagram.

Component Diagram

The main components are:

  • Controllers — Security, Registration, Home, Order, OrderStatus, OrderPayment, Admin, AdminSms, AdminReport, CustomerPortal, Profile, Help.

  • Services — order lifecycle and totals, SMS notifications and reminders, user management.

  • Security — login authenticator, role hierarchy, customer-order voter, CSRF protection.

  • Repositories — users, customers, orders, services, SMS logs.

  • Notifier — outbox transport, transport factory, options.

  • Console commands — demo seeder and unclaimed-reminder command.

  • Templates — base layout, order pages, admin pages, customer portal, help, security, profile.

Figure 15 - Component Diagram.

User Interface

The interface uses the shop's green (#0C4A23) and red (#9C001D) brand colors, a responsive layout, and role-based navigation. The main screens are:

  1. Login — email/password, CSRF-protected.

  2. Owner dashboard — totals, sales cards, laundry-on-hand counts, recent orders.

  3. Order list — search by code/name/phone; filters for status, payment, and date.

  4. Order form — customer, service, weight, loads, clothing item counts, add-ons, instructions, total preview.

  5. Order detail — status timeline, payment badge, item counts, one-click status buttons, receipt link.

  6. Thermal receipt — 72 mm print layout with shop header, ticket, items, total, and payment status.

  7. Customer portal — own tickets, status timeline, counts, amount due, auto-refresh.

  8. Sales reports — daily/monthly/custom range, paid vs unpaid, per-day and per-service tables, CSV export.

  9. SMS outbox — sent messages, trigger filter, outage broadcast.

  10. Help/tutorial — role-specific quick-start steps and the future video slot.
