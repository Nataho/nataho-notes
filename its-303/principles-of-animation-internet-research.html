Last updated: Nov 13, 2026

Principles of Animation - Internet Research

Extra details gathered from the internet (Wikipedia: "Twelve basic principles of animation", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation). Complements the vault summaries in Principles of Animation Summary, 12 Principles of Animation and The illusion of motion.

Where the 12 principles come from

  • Introduced by Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas in their 1981 book The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation.
  • Based on the work of Disney animators from the 1930s onwards (the so-called "Disney's Nine Old Men"), in their quest to produce more realistic animation.
  • Main purpose: produce an illusion that cartoon characters obey the basic laws of physics, but they also deal with abstract issues like emotional timing and character appeal.
  • The book is often called the "Bible of animation"; in 1999 it was voted the best animation book of all time in an Animation World Network online poll.
  • Originally for traditional hand-drawn animation, but still fully relevant to modern computer animation.

The 12 principles in detail

1. Squash and stretch

Gives a sense of weight and flexibility to drawn or computer-animated objects. Works on simple objects (a bouncing ball) or complex ones (the musculature of a human face). Taken to an extreme it becomes comical. Key rule: an object's volume does not change when squashed or stretched - if a ball stretches vertically, its width/depth must contract correspondingly.

2. Anticipation

Prepares the audience for an action and makes the action look more realistic. Examples: a dancer bends the knees before jumping; a golfer swings the club back first; a character looks off-screen to anticipate someone's arrival, or eyes focus on an object the character is about to pick up.

3. Staging

Like staging in theatre and film. Its purpose is to direct the audience's attention and make clear what is most important in a scene. Johnston and Thomas defined it as "the presentation of any idea so that it is completely and unmistakably clear" - whether the idea is an action, a personality, an expression, or a mood. Done through placement of the character in the frame, light and shadow, camera angle and position. Keep focus on what is relevant and avoid unnecessary detail.

4. Straight ahead action and pose to pose

Two approaches to the drawing process:

  • Straight ahead: animated frame by frame from beginning to end. Creates a more fluid, dynamic illusion of movement - better for realistic action sequences - but it is hard to maintain proportions and exact poses.
  • Pose to pose: start with key frames, then fill in the intervals later. Better for dramatic or emotional scenes where composition and relation to surroundings matter.
  • A combination of the two is often used. In computer animation, "pose to pose" is still used for its compositional advantages; computers can fill in the in-between sequences automatically, but the animator must still oversee it and apply the other principles.

5. Follow through and overlapping action

Two closely related techniques that render movement more realistically and make characters obey the laws of physics, including inertia:

  • Follow through: loosely tied parts of a body keep moving after the character has stopped, moving beyond the stopping point before being "pulled back" toward the center of mass, with various degrees of oscillation damping.
  • Overlapping action: parts of the body move at different rates (an arm moves on different timing than the head).
  • A third related technique is drag: when a character starts to move, some parts take a few frames to catch up. Can be clothing, a car antenna, hair, arms, etc. On the human body the torso is the core; arms, legs, head and hair follow its movement. Fleshy parts (stomachs, loose skin) move more independently than bony parts.
  • Related idea: the "moving hold" - animating between two very similar positions so even a sitting character shows some movement (breathing, slight shifts), preventing the animation from looking lifeless.

6. Slow in and slow out

Real-world objects need time to accelerate and slow down. More pictures (frames) are drawn near the beginning and end of an action, fewer in the middle, to emphasize faster action. Emphasizes the object's extreme poses. Applies to characters moving between two extremes (sitting down, standing up) and to inanimate objects like a bouncing ball.

7. Arc

Most natural action follows an arched trajectory, so animation should follow implied "arcs" for greater realism - a rotating limb at a joint, or a thrown object on a parabolic path. The exception is mechanical movement, which typically moves in straight lines. As speed or momentum increases, arcs flatten out (a fastball travels straighter; a fast figure skater can't turn as sharply). Motion that leaves its natural arc for no reason looks erratic instead of fluid - e.g. a pointing fingertip must follow a logical arc between extreme poses. Traditional animators lightly draw the arc on the paper as reference, to be erased later.

8. Secondary action

Adding secondary actions to the main action gives the scene more life and supports the main action: a walking person swings their arms, keeps them in their pockets, whistles, or shows emotion through facial expressions. The key rule: secondary actions must emphasize, not steal attention from the main action - if they would, leave them out. During a dramatic movement facial expressions often go unnoticed, so put them at the beginning and end of the movement rather than during it.

9. Timing

The number of drawings or frames for a given action, which translates to the speed of the action on film. Correct timing makes objects obey the laws of physics: a lightweight object reacts faster to a push than a heavy one. Timing is critical for establishing a character's mood, emotion and reaction, and communicates aspects of a character's personality.

10. Exaggeration

Useful because perfectly realistic motion can look static and dull. The level depends on whether you want realism or a style (caricature, a specific artist's style). The classical Disney definition: remain true to reality, just present it in a wilder, more extreme form. Other forms involve the supernatural or surreal, altered physical features, or story elements. Use restraint: when a scene has several elements, exaggerate them in balance with each other so the viewer isn't confused or overawed.

11. Solid drawing

Means taking into account forms in three-dimensional space, giving them volume and weight. The animator needs to understand 3D shapes, anatomy, weight, balance, light and shadow - classically achieved through art classes and life drawing. Johnston and Thomas warned against creating "twins": characters whose left and right sides mirror each other, which looks lifeless and dull. In computer animation, animators draw less, but their work still benefits greatly from understanding these principles (John Lasseter's 1987 SIGGRAPH paper applied them to 3D computer animation).

12. Appeal

Appeal in a cartoon character corresponds to charisma in an actor. An appealing character is not necessarily sympathetic - villains and monsters can also be appealing. What matters is that the viewer feels the character is real and interesting. Tricks: for likable characters, a symmetrical or baby-like face tends to work; a complicated or hard-to-read face lacks appeal ('captivation') in the pose or character design.
