Systems Thinking Basics: What Are Systems?

1. Core Definition of a System

A system is a group of interacting, interrelated, or interdependent components that form a complex and unified whole.

  • Physical Components: Tangible items like machinery, body organs, or physical parts.

  • Intangible Components: Non-physical elements like processes, relationships, company policies, information flows, and internal states of mind (feelings, values, beliefs).

  • Embedded Networks: Systems exist as nodes inside larger networks. For example, an R&D department is a system within a company, which is a system within an industry, which is a system within an economy.

2. The 5 Defining Characteristics of Systems

To determine whether something is a functional system or merely a collection, it must exhibit these five properties:

1. Complete Integrity

A system’s parts must all be present for it to carry out its purpose optimally.

  • Collection: Removing a cashew from a bowl of mixed nuts leaves fewer nuts, but does not alter the essential nature of the bowl.

  • System: Redefining jobs or removing team members in an R&D department directly alters the system’s performance and relationships.

  • Key Rule: Dividing a system destroys its function ("If you divide an elephant in half, you don't get two smaller elephants").

2. Specific Arrangement

Parts must be organized in a specific structure.

  • In a fruit bowl (collection), swapping the position of apples and oranges changes nothing.

  • In a company (system), randomly swapping accounting specialists with assembly line workers disrupts the entire organization.

3. Purpose Within Larger Systems

Every system serves a discrete purpose in relation to the broader system in which it is embedded. This purpose gives the system its individual integrity.

4. Stability Through Fluctuation (Homeostasis)

Systems maintain stability through continuous internal adjustments and feedback loops.

  • Body temperature fluctuates around 98.6°F based on exertion and sweat response.

  • A company’s profit margins fluctuate weekly as bills are paid and invoices collected, maintaining an average baseline over time.

5. Reliance on Feedback

Feedback is the transmission and return of information that triggers a change in behavior.

  • Immediate Feedback: Steering a car into a turn gives instant visual cues to adjust the wheel.

  • Delayed Feedback: Decisions like employee layoffs may yield immediate financial cost-cutting, but delayed negative feedback (burned-out staff, low morale, high turnover, reduced quality) takes months or years to manifest.

4. The Events / Patterns / Structure Pyramid

Systemic understanding is divided into three distinct levels. The deeper down the pyramid you go, the higher your leverage for long-term change.

       /\
      /  \     [ Events ]      -> React
     /----\
    /      \    [ Patterns ]    -> Adapt
   /--------\
  /          \  [ Structure ]   -> Create Change
 /------------\

1. Events (The Tip)

  • What it is: A single snapshot moment in time (e.g., a building catches fire, a project misses a deadline).

  • Action Mode: React!

  • Limitation: Solving problems at this level yields only short-lived fixes and does not stop recurrence.

2. Patterns (The Middle)

  • What it is: Trends or recurring changes in events over time (e.g., sales declining over 3 years, high engineer turnover over 6 months).

  • Action Mode: Adapt!

  • Benefit: Allows you to anticipate trends and plan responses based on historical behavior.

3. Systemic Structure (The Base)

  • What it is: The invisible web of interrelationships and causal connections driving the patterns.

  • Action Mode: Create Change!

  • Benefit: Offers high-leverage, self-sustaining solutions (e.g., implementing building fire codes and sprinkler policies instead of just putting out fires).

5. Systemic Diagramming & Causal Loops

Why Diagram Systems?

Because all systems are part of larger open networks, no diagram can capture 100% of a system. However, creating Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs) allows individuals and groups to externalize mental models, surface hidden assumptions, and identify leverage points for systemic changes.

Example: The Engineering Exodus Loop

When probing structure, look for circular feedback chains rather than linear cause-and-effect:

  1. Budget Cuts lead to Administrative Staff Layoffs.

  2. Fewer admins increase remaining Engineers' Workloads.

  3. High workloads damage Morale.

  4. Low morale leads to Engineers Leaving.

  5. Staff loss increases Workloads even further for those left behind, reinforcing the downward spiral.
