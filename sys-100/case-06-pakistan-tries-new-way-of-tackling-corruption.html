Pakistan Tries New Way of Tackling Corruption

#SYS100#case-study#systems-thinking#corruption#governanceLast updated: Oct 27, 2026

Case 06 — Pakistan Tries New Way of Tackling Corruption

Summary

In Punjab, Pakistan's most populous province, an anti-corruption program harnesses ubiquitous cell phones to proactively ask citizens whether they were asked for a bribe, then uses the data to discipline officials. Experts call it the first large-scale government effort to solicit such feedback and act on it. The article weighs its promise against the deep patronage politics that make petty bribery the currency of the system.

Storyline

  1. The corruption baseline: bribery is so pervasive that even Osama bin Laden paid a bribe to build his hideout; ordinary Pakistanis grease palms to file police reports, obtain court documents, and visit relatives in hospitals.
  2. The intervention: enterprising officials in Punjab, the most populous province, launch a program that harnesses ubiquitous cell phones to identify corruption hot spots — the first large-scale attempt by any government to proactively solicit feedback from bribery victims and use it to discipline officials.
  3. The theory of change (Nabeel Awan): the deterrent is knowing "there is someone watching" who can hold you to what the public says — "it may not eliminate corruption, but it does reduce corruption and bad administration."
  4. The stakes: the anti-corruption wing estimates graft costs billions of dollars a year; citizens rank corruption among the nation's biggest problems and it's worsening — Pakistan slipped nine places to the 33rd most corrupt of 176 countries (Transparency International).
  5. The political stage: spring parliamentary elections approach, watched closely by Washington; cricket star Imran Khan campaigns on anti-graft, squeezing the ruling PPP and the opposition PML-N — which runs Punjab and may hope the program (fully operational mid-2012) steals Khan's thunder.
  6. How it runs: the Punjab Information Technology Board uses robocalls and text messages to survey citizens transacting with a dozen departments — property, health, and emergency response among them.
  7. The case files: most reports involve low-level property officials called patwaris — a Multan man paid ~$170 to register new property, a Sheikhpura man ~$15 with a demand "they should be removed from their jobs," bin Laden's courier paid ~$500 for the required land in Abbottabad, a Multan man was asked ~$300 to register a police case, and a Lahore woman faced bribes just to visit relatives in hospital.
  8. The workflow: government clerks must log the phone numbers of citizens they serve; Punjab's chief minister Shahbaz Sharif robocalls each citizen, warning a text survey is coming; responses land in a computer database, and call-center agents chase non-responders in a country where literacy is near 50 percent.
  9. The numbers so far: over 1 million citizens contacted, ~175,000 responding; about 6,000 — 1 in 29 — reported corruption, and 18,000+ reported other complaints; the low corruption rate may reflect reluctance to tell officials the truth (Callen, UCLA), which could improve as anonymity and punishment take hold.
  10. The contrast: India's "I Paid a Bribe" website relies on citizens initiating complaints — which can produce fictional accusations to blackmail honest officials; Punjab's proactive solicitation sidesteps that (Umar Saif).
  11. The enforcement teeth: the chief minister's office sent Rawalpindi's top official 100+ corruption reports on a residency-certificate office, resulting in suspended clerks; officials who falsified citizens' phone numbers to dodge oversight were punished.
  12. The counterweight: Lahore's top political official Mengal worries bribes are so ingrained the bureaucracy might "seize up" — a threatened official "may just delay the transaction" — leaving open whether there's political will to follow through.

Key Points

  • Scale of the problem: corruption costs Pakistan billions of dollars a year; the country slipped nine places to the 33rd most corrupt of 176 countries (Transparency International). Even Osama bin Laden's courier paid a ~$500 bribe to a land official (patwari) to build the Abbottabad compound.
  • How it works: government clerks must log citizens' phone numbers; citizens get a robocall from Punjab's chief minister Shahbaz Sharif, then a text asking if they paid a bribe. Responses are logged in a database; call-center agents follow up with non-responders (literacy is near 50%).
  • Results so far: 1 million+ citizens contacted, ~175,000 responded; ~6,000 (1 in 29) reported corruption, 18,000+ reported other complaints. Over 100 corruption reports on a Rawalpindi residency-certificate office led to clerks being suspended; officials caught falsifying phone numbers were punished.
  • Why it's different: unlike India's citizen-initiated "I Paid a Bribe" website, proactive solicitation avoids fictional accusations used to blackmail honest officials. Its strength is deterrence — officials know someone is watching.
  • Limits: doesn't touch high-level corruption; low reporting may reflect fear of telling officials the truth; jobs are coveted because of bribe opportunities, not salaries; one Lahore official worried that pressured clerks would simply delay transactions rather than stop taking bribes.
  • Political context: comes ahead of spring elections, with cricket star Imran Khan campaigning hard on anti-graft.

Analysis Questions (from the case)

  1. Identify what "official bribery" influences and what influences it. Quantify the variables identified.
  2. Develop a reinforcing loop.
  3. Create a balancing loop on top of the existing loop. Evaluate its significance.
